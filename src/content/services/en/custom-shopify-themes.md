## What makes the difference two years later

Two themes can look identical on delivery day. On one, the client builds a landing page in twenty minutes; on the other, they ask for a quote every time they want to change a line of copy. The difference is in how the [sections and blocks](/en/glossary/sections-and-blocks/) were split, and that does not show in the mockup.

My rule: one section per page intention, not per row of the design. Three text-and-image bands that differ only in which side the photo is on are one section with a setting, not three sections. And the other way round: one section with forty settings nobody understands is split just as badly.

## What is always included

- **Sections with sensible defaults**, which look right before anything is configured.
- **Capped blocks**, because a carousel that accepts thirty blocks will end up with thirty.
- **Copy in the settings**, not in the code, and names in your team's language.
- **Images with declared dimensions** and the main one loaded early, which is half of performance.
- **Whatever repeats across pages in [metaobjects](/en/glossary/metaobjects/)**: the client edits it once and it changes on 200 product pages.

## Performance from the start

A fast theme is not achieved by optimising at the end. It is achieved by not introducing the problem: not loading the main image inside a carousel that boots with JavaScript, not walking entire collections in [Liquid](/en/glossary/liquid/) to calculate something that fits in a metafield, and not repainting the cart by hand when you can ask for the rendered section.

In [projects](/en/projects/type/custom-themes/) there are three themes built this way, and on [the code page](/en/code/) are this site's own numbers, measured against the deployed site.
